# Licensing, in plain English

The formal terms are in [LICENSE.md](LICENSE.md) — the **PolyForm Small Business License 1.0.0**,
a standard licence written by licensing lawyers. This page explains what it means. Where the two
disagree, the licence text wins.

## The videos you make are yours. Completely.

This is the part that matters most, so it comes first.

**Kite's licence applies to Kite — the software — and not to anything you create with it.** Your
footage is yours. Your edits are yours. The exported file is yours. Sell it, monetise it, licence
it to a client, put it behind a paywall, use it in an advert. Kite claims nothing, asks for
nothing, and imposes no conditions on your output. There is no watermark and no attribution
requirement on your videos.

A restriction on commercial *use of the editor* would be pointless if it stopped a creator
earning from their own work. It does not.

## Who can use Kite free of charge

**Free, with no permission needed:**

- Anyone using it personally — hobby projects, learning, family videos, whatever you like
- Creators and freelancers, including ones earning a living from what they make
- Students, teachers and schools
- Charities, non-profits and community groups
- Companies with **fewer than 100 people** and **less than $1,000,000 of revenue** last tax year

That covers essentially every individual and small team.

**Needs a separate licence from us:**

- Companies at or above either threshold — 100+ people, or $1M+ revenue

If that is you and you would like to use Kite, open an issue or get in touch. The intent is not to
be difficult; it is that a company of that size getting commercial value from the tool should
contribute to it, while everyone else uses it for nothing.

## What you can do with the code

You can read it, build it, change it, and share your changes — for any purpose the licence
permits. If you pass Kite on to someone else, include these terms with it.

## Two honest caveats

**This is source-available, not open source.** Because it treats large companies differently, it
does not meet the Open Source Initiative's definition, which forbids discriminating between fields
of use. GitHub will label it "View license" rather than naming a recognised licence, and some
Linux distributions will not package it. That is the deliberate trade for the restriction, and it
is worth knowing rather than discovering later.

**Kite bundles FFmpeg, which has its own licence.** Kite runs FFmpeg as a separate program rather
than building it in, so the two licences do not conflict — but the FFmpeg binaries we ship stay
under their own terms and we have obligations for them. See
[THIRD-PARTY.md](THIRD-PARTY.md).

## Changing your mind later

Nothing here is permanent. As the copyright holder you can relicense future versions however you
want — including making it fully open source. What you cannot do is retract the licence on
versions already released. Starting more restrictive and loosening later is much easier than the
reverse, which is a reasonable argument for beginning here.
