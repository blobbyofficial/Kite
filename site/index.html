A video editor that stays fast on a modest laptop

Most editors get slow exactly when you need them most: scrubbing a long clip, trimming frame by frame, cutting a talking-head take down to size. Kite is built so those things stay instant on ordinary hardware.

01 Why you might want this

Editing a twenty-minute video on a five-year-old laptop is miserable in most tools. You drag the playhead and the picture catches up a second later. You trim a clip and the whole window hitches. The usual advice is to make proxies yourself, in a submenu, before you start.

Kite does that for you, automatically, the moment you import — and it is the reason everything else feels immediate.

Scrubbing

Seeking is not a special case

Jump to any frame, anywhere in a file, and it costs the same as playing the next one. No waiting for the picture to resolve, no guessing where you are.

Long timelines

Hour two feels like minute one

Cost is driven by what is on screen, not by how much is on the timeline. A ten-thousand clip sequence responds as quickly as an empty one.

Quality

Edit on proxies, export the originals

The fast playback files are for editing only. Export reads your original footage, so the file you upload is full quality no matter what you scrubbed against.

Nothing to set up

Import is instant

A clip is editable about a second after you add it, however long it is, because the playback files are prepared as you go rather than up front. ffmpeg is bundled: no codec packs, no account, no cloud, no telemetry.

02 How it actually works

Camera and screen-capture footage is nearly always long-GOP H.264 or HEVC: most frames are stored as differences from earlier ones. To show frame 4,000 the decoder may have to walk through hundreds of frames first. That is the stutter you feel when you scrub.

Kite converts the footage into an indexed format where every frame stands alone, so finding a frame is a lookup in a table. Crucially it does this only for the parts you actually visit: the file is divided into short spans and a span is prepared the moment the timeline asks for a frame inside it. Opening a two-hour recording costs nothing, and jumping an hour in prepares that one span, not the hour before it.

Three other things that keep it responsive

Threading

Nothing heavy on the interface

Decoding, importing and exporting all run on background threads. The window shows the newest frame it already has rather than freezing until the right one arrives.

Drawing

Only what you can see

Clips, waveforms and thumbnails outside the visible part of the timeline are never touched, so scrolling stays flat regardless of sequence length.

Audio

Sound is the clock

Playback is driven by the audio output, which never blocks or allocates. If the picture cannot keep up, sound stays continuous instead of glitching.

Editing

Undo is instant

The whole project is snapshotted on each edit in about a fifth of a millisecond, so undo and redo never stall, even on a very long sequence.

03 Cutting your first video

  1. Drag your footage onto the window. Kite prepares each file in the background — you can start working before it finishes.

  2. Double-click a clip in the media list to put it on the timeline.

  3. Press S to cut at the playhead. Select a bad take and press Shift+Del to remove it and close the gap.

  4. Drag a clip's edge to trim it, or its middle to move it — including onto another track for b-roll.

  5. Add a title with the T button, then edit the wording, size, colour and position in the inspector. Select a clip and press Ctrl+T to dissolve into it from the one before.

  6. Press Ctrl+M to queue a render, set where it should go, then press Render. Kite uses your graphics card's encoder if it has one.

04 What it does, and what it doesn't

This is a first beta and it is deliberately narrow. It does the cutting work well and leaves the rest for later, rather than doing everything badly.

AreaIn this build
TimelineMulti-track video and audio, trim, ripple delete, split, drag between tracks, snapping, rubber-band select, copy and paste, duplicate, nudge
PictureOpacity, scale, position, fades, crossfades between clips, titles with colour and alignment
ColourBrightness, contrast and saturation per clip, with one-click looks
Speed0.25× to 4×, with the audio retimed to match
AudioPer-clip volume in dB, fades, waveforms, level meters
ProjectProject manager, media pool with bins, several timelines per project, undo/redo, autosave with crash recovery
RenderingA render queue: per-row output settings and destination, rendered in order
ExportH.264 MP4 from your original media, hardware encoding when available
GradingNot yet — no curves, wheels or scopes
EffectsNot yet — no keyframes, and crossfade is the only transition
OtherWindows only for now. No 3D, no motion graphics

It is a beta, and it is honest about that. The media pipeline — import, decoding, titles, export — is checked automatically on every single build, including an export of a 120-cut timeline. The interface itself has had far less real-world use, so if something is going to be rough, that is where it will be.

Found something broken? Open an issue — that is exactly what this beta is for.

06 Building it yourself

Kite is written in Rust and draws its own interface on the GPU — there is no browser inside it, which is part of why it starts quickly and stays small.

With a Rust toolchain and ffmpeg available, cargo build --release is the whole thing. kite --selftest runs the full pipeline check against a synthesised clip and prints the timings you see on this page.
